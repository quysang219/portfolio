Compliance is a product surface, not a binder.

The journey behind the platforms — two problems and two solutions across four years, and how a governance role turned into a security-engineering practice.

The arc

Two problems, two solutions.

Click a stage to read it. Each problem made the next solution obvious; each solution made the next problem visible.

Stage 01 · The problem

Security work that lived in Excel.

I joined VPBank's IT Security team in 2022 as a Governance Officer expecting policy work. The bottleneck wasn't policy — the program had policies. It needed infrastructure: a place for ownership to live, a way for evidence to survive a quarter, a surface where the asset owner could act without paging anyone.

My day-to-day was vulnerability management. Every cycle was hand-offs with no system in the middle holding state:

The original loop
Scan → export → split per owner → password-wrap → email → wait → re-scan → verify by eye

The numbers in the monthly report were never the numbers the scanner saw that morning. The owners didn't know what was theirs until the email arrived. The security team spent more time ferrying findings around than thinking about them.

Stage 02 · The solution

A pipeline, and a dashboard the team actually opened.

A data pipeline ingesting scan results directly, joined to the asset inventory, landed in Postgres as snapshot history. A Power BI report on top — each owner saw a view filtered to their own scope: what's vulnerable, what's overdue, what's closed, what the trend looks like.

The hand-offs collapsed. No more password-wrapped files in inboxes — the owner opened a link and saw the live state. The monthly report stopped being a manual tally; it was a refresh of the same model the operators were already using.

The shift, in one line: we stopped being the messenger between the scanner and the owner. The dashboard was the messenger. We became the people who designed what it said.

Stage 03 · The next problem

Visibility isn't action.

The dashboard told the owner what was wrong. It didn't help the expert close the loop on the work that came back. Three things still lived outside the system, and all three were load-bearing:

Bottleneck · 01
Notes, plans, exceptions — by hand
Justified delays, agreed remediation plans, approved exceptions — all captured in separate documents. The dashboard couldn't reflect any of it.
Bottleneck · 02
Progress, tracked from memory
"Is this team actually moving, quarter over quarter?" was a judgement call from whoever had read the dashboard the longest. No history of state, only the latest snapshot.
Bottleneck · 03
Anomalies, quietly suspected
Experts noticed that "compliant" sometimes meant "the sensor stopped reporting." The dashboards couldn't tell the difference. The concern lived in people's heads.

Each of these was a place where the human had to do the system's job — because the system had no slot to hold it.

Stage 04 · The next solution

An operator surface.

A self-service surface on the same Postgres store. Owners stopped emailing notes; they typed them into the platform against the specific finding. Plans, exceptions, delegations — captured in-platform, routed to the right approver, time-bounded, visible to everyone who needed to see them.

Two pieces of leverage for the experts: history tracking turned "is this team moving?" into a chart anyone could read; routed comments with AI assist handled the high-volume, low-novelty replies, so expert attention went to the cases that actually needed it.

The silent-failure problem solved itself once history was there. "Compliant for 90 days running" looked different from "compliant since the agent went quiet 87 days ago." We flagged those, traced several to vendor-side issues, and got them fixed at source.

Zoom-in · one small decision

The shape of the table is the audit story.

Why the ownership table is append-only

A choice that paid for itself twelve months later.

When the program needed an ownership graph, the obvious shape was a mutable mapping — (asset → owner), updated in place. Cleaner queries, smaller storage, the standard pattern.

I built it append-only instead: (asset, owner, valid_from, valid_to, action, actor). Three reasons, only one of which was obvious up front.

One — audit becomes free. "Who owned SRV-PROD-04 on 12 March, and who granted that?" is a single SELECT, not a forensic project across backup tapes. Two — concurrent grants and revokes are safe. Inserts are atomic; the lock contention that mutates create disappears. Three — and this is the unobvious one — every downstream report that filters by ownership now inherits point-in-time correctness without any extra logic. The dashboard for last quarter shows last-quarter's owners, automatically.

The mutable version would have shipped faster. It would also have forced a second system to retrofit history into it twelve months later, after the first auditor asked.

The model we ended up with

Four layers, one product.

Looking back, the two solutions stack into one shape — each layer feeding the one above. Nothing was designed up front; the layers emerged because each missing one became, in turn, the next bottleneck.

L01
Snapshot truth
Every relevant signal lands in Postgres as an append-only history. What was true on Day N stays true on Day N — forever.
L02
Owner surface
The asset owner has one place to see their scope and act on it — request, justify, renew, delegate — without a ticket queue.
L03
Audit trail
Every action writes back to the same store the dashboards read. The audit log is a by-product, not a separate system.
L04
Executive read
Leadership reports are the last derivation, not the first build. Fix the model below; every report inherits the fix.
Snapshot → Surface → Audit → Executive

Most security reporting goes the other way — start from the executive slide, work backward to the spreadsheet, and treat the owner as a passive subject. That model produces beautiful PDFs and 90-day audit fire drills. This one produces a system the owner actually opens.

Principles

How I work.

  • Closed loops, not one-shot reports. Every metric should be actionable by the owner in the same tool that surfaces it. Otherwise it's a screenshot.
  • Snapshot the truth, not yesterday's screenshot. Append-only history is the cheapest auditor in the room — and the only one that scales past the second financial year.
  • The dashboard is downstream of the data model. Fix the schema once; every report inherits the fix. The reverse path doesn't work, no matter how hard you DAX at it.
  • Self-service beats ticketing. If the owner can answer their own question, the program scales. If they can't, you've just hired a queue.
