About

Pipelined Compliance.

As infrastructure and applications scale by the seconds, how do we reliably detect and protect?

Over the years, I observed that security requirements reached System Owners through multiple fragmented channels, with execution managed through emails, chats, and spreadsheets. Escalations and audits frequently occurred without the full context of related security activities across domains, making it difficult to understand, quantify, and continuously measure an organization's true security posture. As infrastructure becomes increasingly agile, elastic, and automated, the way we secure it must evolve just as rapidly.

To address these challenges, my work has focused on transforming fragmented security operations into a connected, data-driven ecosystem. By partnering with security teams, I capture their requirements, engineer them into scalable workflows, connect activities across security domains, build analytics capabilities that surface context instantly, create a unified platform for System Owners, and expose meaningful metrics that make security posture measurable, transparent, and actionable for managers.

Scaling compliance through self-service

Quarterly vulnerability scanning is a regulatory requirement for us (Circular 09), but a mandate doesn't organize itself. Findings arrived in bulk, every team asked "is mine fixed?", and security became the bottleneck for verification.

We built the process end-to-end: risk-based prioritization, remediation SLAs by tier, and progress tracking that made ownership unambiguous. The key unlock was self-service rescan initiation — teams could verify their own fixes on demand instead of queuing behind us, which turned remediation from a ping-pong of emails into a loop teams could close themselves.

Result: 95% compliance sustained across quarterly cycles, with security steering the process rather than pushing every ticket.

Demonstrates: process design, automation, removing yourself as the bottleneck.

When the monitoring tool is the anomaly

An endpoint security agent only protects you if it's actually running — and reporting truthfully. Our coverage dashboard said one thing; reality said another.

We drove agent compliance across the server fleet, but the interesting work was in the gaps. We built a writeback portal so teams could log exception explanations directly instead of burying them in email threads, giving us an auditable record of every uncovered machine and why. Digging into the residual anomalies, we found some weren't the teams' fault at all — the agent itself was misbehaving — and we escalated and pushed the fix rather than letting those cases rot as "non-compliant."

Result: 95% compliance, with the remaining 5% explained and documented rather than unknown.

Demonstrates: data skepticism, root-cause analysis, building for auditability.

You can't protect data you don't know exists

Cardholder data has a way of escaping its designated zone — a debug log here, an ad-hoc export there. We ran continuous sensitive-data discovery scans across our servers to find card data living where it shouldn't.

We made this a managed process rather than a one-off audit: tracking remediation of known findings with system owners, and flagging newly appearing cases early — because a new finding usually means something upstream is leaking and will keep leaking until addressed.

Result: a substantial volume of exposed card data found and remediated with system owners — directly strengthening our PCI DSS posture, with new exposures now caught as they appear rather than at the next annual audit.

Demonstrates: data protection mindset, continuous monitoring over point-in-time audits.

From a twice-yearly sample to daily full coverage

Firewall rulebases only grow — every project adds rules, few remove them. Reviews used to cover a limited group of rules twice a year; by consolidating rule data and centralizing processing, the team now reviews an incomparably larger set of rules daily. A writeback portal gives the process a clear workflow — system-owner comment, security review, then action or logged exception — with AI assisting the security-review step to cut through volume.

My role is the analytics and communication layer: surfacing which teams are accumulating findings and where progress is stalling, and drafting the communications that keep owners moving.

Demonstrates: data-driven prioritization, knowing where you fit in a larger machine.

Raising review frequency by removing the manual ceiling

Account reviews used to be as frequent as the manual effort allowed: consolidating extracts in Excel, chasing responses, assembling the picture by hand. That ceiling meant reviews happened rarely — and stale accounts and privileges lived longer between them.

We replaced the manual consolidation with an automatically updated dashboard, so the current state of accounts is always visible instead of reconstructed each cycle. The review itself became something we could run far more frequently, shrinking the window in which a dormant account or excessive privilege goes unnoticed.

Result: from periodic, effort-bound reviews built on manual Excel consolidation to an always-current dashboard — review frequency limited by policy choice, not by manual capacity.

Demonstrates: access governance, automating away the bottleneck that limits cadence.

Remediating a decade of history without breaking it

Hardening a freshly built server is easy. Hardening hundreds of servers that carry years of historical configuration is not — a config change that's "obviously correct" can silently break an application that has depended on the old behavior since before anyone can remember.

So the process we ran treated every fix as a change to be tested and impact-controlled, not a checkbox. Where remediation wasn't feasible, the requirement was to say so formally: we built a portal for teams to log remediation plans and exceptions, plus trend reporting that showed leadership real progress over time instead of a static snapshot.

Result: 95% compliance across the estate, with every deviation documented — no silent gaps.

Demonstrates: pragmatic risk management, respect for production, long-horizon execution.

Making endpoint compliance visible enough to act on

"Are our workstations secure?" is unanswerable until you define it. The fleet is managed against nine concrete criteria — security agent health, OS patching, antivirus signature currency, and more — and my contribution was making that legible: visualizing coverage across all nine criteria so that a machine falling behind is a visible, assignable fact rather than a surprise during an incident.

That visibility became operational, not just informational: GRC and the Service Desk use it in their ticket-approval process, checking an endpoint's compliance state before granting requests.

Demonstrates: turning raw compliance data into a tool other teams rely on.

Replacing an annual fire drill with year-round assurance

Every year, PCI DSS compliance meant a vendor's tool scanning a sample of 30 servers, manually extracting results, and chasing teams for fixes and exceptions — painful, slow, and it only ever proved 30 servers were compliant.

I didn't want to just survive the ritual; I wanted to make it obsolete. We used our internal scanning platform to build a mirror of the vendor's checks, validated the equivalence and got the vendor's agreement on the approach, then folded PCI hardening into the same streamlined workflow we'd built for vulnerabilities — portals, tracking, exceptions and all.

Result: from an annual 30-server sample to proactive, continuous compliance across the entire server fleet — the audit became a formality because we already knew the answer.

Demonstrates: initiative, vendor negotiation, converting audits into engineering.

Turning the click rate into a lever, not a scoreboard

A phishing simulation that only produces a click percentage teaches nobody anything. We tracked each campaign end-to-end — not just who clicked, but who reported it — and followed repeat clickers across campaigns so awareness effort could target the people and departments that actually needed it.

The reporting turned a compliance exercise into a trend leadership could act on: susceptibility by department over time, and whether the report rate — the signal that people are becoming part of the defense — was moving in the right direction.

Demonstrates: behavioral analytics, turning awareness metrics into targeted action.

Turning security noise into a picture leadership can act on

Firewalls, WAF, email gateways, DLP — each produces its own flood of events, and raw floods don't inform decisions. We produced the consolidated threat reporting: attacks blocked at the perimeter and WAF, phishing campaigns targeting our users, DLP incidents, blocked malicious mail, and takedowns of fake domains impersonating our brand.

The craft here is editorial: not "here are ten charts," but what changed, what's trending, what deserves attention — a coherent narrative of the threat landscape that lets leadership see the organization the way an attacker does.

Demonstrates: synthesis, security communication, seeing the whole battlefield.
